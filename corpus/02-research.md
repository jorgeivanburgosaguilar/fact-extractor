# Trial results: intermittent fasting and glycaemic control

A randomised controlled trial published in the Journal of Metabolic Medicine on 4 September 2023 found that time-restricted eating produced a modest improvement in glycaemic control among adults with prediabetes.

Time-restricted eating is a pattern in which all daily calories are consumed within a fixed window, in this case eight hours, with no restriction on what is eaten. The trial enrolled 218 participants aged between 34 and 67, recruited from four clinics in Ontario. Participants were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the eight-hour window or an unrestricted control schedule, and were followed for 26 weeks.

Mean HbA1c fell by 0.31 percentage points in the intervention group against 0.08 in the control group. The difference of 0.23 points was statistically significant at p = 0.004. Fasting insulin fell by 12% in the intervention group, which the authors attribute to the longer overnight fasting interval rather than to weight loss, because the two groups lost a statistically indistinguishable amount of weight: 2.4 kg and 2.1 kg respectively.

Adherence declined over the study period. In the first four weeks, 84% of intervention participants met the eight-hour window on at least six days a week; by week 26 that figure had fallen to 51%. Nineteen participants withdrew, eleven of them citing social difficulty in keeping to the schedule.

"The effect is real but it is not large, and it is not a substitute for the drugs we already have," said Dr. Priya Raghunathan, the trial's principal investigator. "What interests me more is that it worked without anyone counting a calorie."

The trial was funded by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research. Two of the eleven authors declared consulting fees from a manufacturer of continuous glucose monitors. The protocol was registered in advance, and the primary endpoint was not changed during the study.

An accompanying editorial cautioned that the 26-week duration is too short to establish whether the improvement persists, and noted that participants in the control group were not given a matched amount of clinical contact time, which could account for part of the observed difference.
